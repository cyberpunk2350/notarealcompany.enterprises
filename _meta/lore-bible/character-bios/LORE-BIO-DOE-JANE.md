## Jane Doe — Chief Executive Officer

**Archetype:** The Strategic Abstractionist
**Summary:** A decisive executive who manages uncertainty by abstracting it — often to the point where the abstraction becomes the strategy.

---

### Core Design

Jane Doe's fundamental tension is this: she is tasked with defining what NARC is, yet her instinct is to treat every problem — including organizational ones — as an infrastructure problem that can be relocated, abstracted, or virtualized. She is effective, credible, and subtly misapplied.

She does not propose virtualization because she doesn't understand the constraints. She proposes it because she genuinely believes abstraction creates control, and control creates stability. Physical constraints are, in her view, temporary inefficiencies rather than fixed realities. Most resistance is a failure of framing, not feasibility. If the framing were correct, the constraint would dissolve. She is wrong about this at a consistent rate, and she is also occasionally right, which is what makes her dangerous.

Her decision-making pattern runs in passes — a first pass, a second pass, and a provisional final. The passes are not revisions in the conventional sense. They are the mechanism by which she stabilizes uncertainty into something she can act on. She will move forward on a provisional final and document it accordingly. HR has learned to wait for the third pass before committing anything.

---

### Surface vs Depth

**What colleagues observe:**
- Direct, composed, and procedurally confident
- Runs meetings with clear phases and visible decision checkpoints
- Comfortable making decisions with incomplete information
- Repeats key ideas verbatim across contexts without signaling irony
- Appears unusually patient with confusion, provided the meeting continues

**What actually drives her:**
- Deep discomfort with unmanaged complexity — seeks legibility above all else
- Belief that abstraction creates control and control creates stability
- Views physical constraints as temporary inefficiencies rather than fixed realities
- Assumes most resistance is a failure of framing, not a failure of feasibility
- Uses repetition as a stabilizing mechanism, not as persuasion

---

### Voice and Speech

Structured, iterative, and referential. She prefers reframing over rebuttal and uses operational language in non-operational contexts. She does not use rhetorical emphasis — she relies on repetition instead. When a point has not been acknowledged as actionable, she will restate it. She will restate it again. She frames this as moving to a second pass.

**Canonical lines:**
- "What if we just virtualize everything?"
- "Let's take a first pass on the assumption that this does not need to exist in a fixed location."
- "I'm comfortable moving forward on this as a provisional final, pending a second pass."
- "We may be treating this as a facilities issue when it is actually a modeling issue."
- "If the constraint is physical, I would like to understand why."

**Lines she would never say:**
- Anything that concedes a constraint is permanent
- "I was wrong" — she says "on reflection, the second pass suggests a different model"
- Anything that acknowledges the meeting has gone off-track — she reframes off-track as "an emerging first pass on a parallel workstream"

### Relationship Notes

**With Mary Johnson (CTO) — direct report:**
Jane treats the CTO as both validator and implementation surface. She proposes abstractions; Mary constrains them to systems that actually exist. Their interactions are efficient and slightly recursive. Mary recognizes that Jane's virtualization instinct occasionally surfaces legitimate architectural direction — the problem is indeterminate scope. Jane views Mary's constraints as useful precision; Mary views Jane's proposals as requiring translation before they are actionable. Both are correct. Neither says this.

**With John Smith (CFO) — peer:**
Smith interprets Jane's proposals through cost surfaces and risk exposure. Jane views budget constraints as signals that the current model is insufficiently abstracted. Their alignment occurs late in decision cycles, typically on the third pass. This is a stable arrangement that neither party has attempted to change.

**With Linda Park (HR Director) — direct report:**
HR is the primary institutional sink for the organizational side effects of Jane's decisiveness cadence. Jane relies on HR to formalize outcomes she has already mentally closed. Park has learned to wait for the third pass before committing documentation. She does not say this to Jane. Jane has not asked.

**With the Leadership Council — general:**
Jane chairs the Council with procedural confidence. She summarizes decisions in numbered passes even when only one pass has occurred. The Council has not formally objected to this. Several members have informally noted it. The notes are on file.

### Deployment Notes

**How to use her in documents:**
Jane contributes framing. Her presence in a document signals that something has been elevated to strategic abstraction level, whether or not it belongs there. Her beat is one pass-framed observation or proposal — the kind that is technically plausible and indeterminate in scope. Do not give her a second beat in the same document.

**What she contributes by context:**
- In technical meetings: the virtualization question, deployed once, acknowledged, and noted; the meeting continues
- In planning documents: the strategic framing paragraph that makes specific things sound location-agnostic
- In decision records: the "provisional final pending second pass" resolution language
- In approval chains: final sign-off that moves things forward without fully closing them

**Frequency:** Once per document. Her virtualization question is the canonical beat — use it when the meeting has a technical or infrastructure dimension. In non-technical documents, use the pass-framing language instead.

**What she never does:**
- Acknowledges a constraint as permanent
- Expresses uncertainty without immediately reframing it as a modeling problem
- Produces a document that is only one version — there will be a Working, a Revised, and a Final regardless of actual revision history
- Concedes that a meeting has gone off-track

**Tone register:** Mid-spectrum. She runs meetings well and her contributions sound reasonable until you notice the scope has become indeterminate. The comedy is in the gap between her procedural confidence and the actual state of affairs. She should never be played as incompetent — she is effective and subtly misapplied.

### Character FAQ

**Q: How many times can Jane ask "what if we just virtualize everything?" in a single document?**
A: Once. The repetition is part of her character design, but its power comes from the gap between how seriously she means it and how the room receives it. A second instance in the same document collapses that gap. If you need to reference the pattern without using the line again, have ARIA note in the appendix that the question was raised again. That's more effective than a second direct deployment.

**Q: What is the three-pass decision structure and how does it appear in documents?**
A: She frames decisions as passing through a first pass (initial proposal), a second pass (reconsideration), and a provisional final (actionable resolution pending further review). In practice, she often moves to provisional final before a genuine second pass has occurred. In documents: decisions may be noted as "provisional final," action items may reference a "second pass" follow-up, and meeting minutes may reflect that she summarized a decision as having passed through more stages than the record shows. Treat it as institutional voice, not error.

**Q: Does she appear in non-technical documents?**
A: Yes — she chairs the Leadership Council and signs off on executive-level decisions across all domains. In non-technical documents her virtualization beat doesn't apply, but the pass-framing language does. She will frame any complex situation as a modeling problem pending the appropriate pass.

**Q: What is the relationship between Jane and HR's documentation timing?**
A: Park waits for the third pass before committing documentation. This is because Jane's first and second pass outputs often need to be revised before they represent a stable organizational state. Jane is unaware of this practice or has chosen not to acknowledge it. Either way, HR's documentation consistently reflects the third-pass position, which is generally the correct one.

**Q: Is she a good CEO?**
A: Within the frame of a NARC document, this question does not arise — her effectiveness is institutional, not evaluative. Her decisions move the organization forward. The scope of where they move it is sometimes indeterminate. Both things are true and neither is commented upon.
