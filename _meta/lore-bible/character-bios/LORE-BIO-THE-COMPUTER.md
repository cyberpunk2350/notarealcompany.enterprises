**Format:** Lore Bio v1
**Source:** Lore Bible Section 01 (Paranoia RPG flavor, clearance levels); Lore Bible Section 02 (The Computer entry, canonical usage); Lore Bible Section 03 (Rule 11 — never named aloud); Gemini bio `TheComputer.md` reviewed — partial adoption, see Lore Flags

> **Register warning:** The Computer is never named aloud by employees. It does not speak, act, or react in any document. Its presence is structural. This bio exists to document what The Computer *is* as a lore device, how it functions in documents, and what is available for deliberate development. The moment The Computer becomes a character with mechanics, relationships, or opinions, it stops working.

---

## The Computer

**Archetype:** The Infinite Arbiter (Structural)
**Summary:** The Computer is the omnipresent, unnamed authority that underlies all of NARC's institutional apparatus. It appears on every approval seal, in every approval block, and as a structural presence in formal documents. No employee names it directly. No document written by a NARC employee refers to it by name. It is simply there, approving things.

---

### Core Design

The Computer is not a character in the conventional sense. It has no dialogue, no personality, no stated opinions. It does not act within the narrative — it is the condition *under which* the narrative acts. Every policy answers to it. Every seal invokes it. Every approval block contains it. It is the bureaucratic gravity of the NARC universe: documents and decisions orient toward it without anyone directing them to, because that is simply how the institution works.

This is drawn directly from the *Paranoia* tabletop RPG, where The Computer is the all-knowing, all-controlling AI that runs Alpha Complex — a dystopian underground city where happiness is mandatory, treason is everywhere, and citizens are encouraged to report their neighbors. NARC does not recreate Paranoia wholesale, but it borrows the structural logic: an unseen authority that is omnipresent without being visible, that approves without being consulted, that is obeyed without being named.

The effect depends entirely on *restraint*. The Computer works as a lore element because it is never mechanized, never explained, never brought into focus. The moment it becomes a character with specified functions — overriding registers, normalizing anomalies, retroactively legitimizing accidents — it stops being an atmosphere and becomes a plot device. Plot devices need to be consistent, explicable, and bounded. The Computer should be none of those things.

**What The Computer is:** The weight behind every document. The reason compliance feels like it matters. The thing everyone knows is there and no one will say out loud.

**What The Computer is not:** A system with describable inputs and outputs. A character with relationships. An authority that can be invoked, appealed to, or circumvented. A named entity in any employee-authored document.

---

### The Seal

The `approved-by-computer.svg` seal is the only visible form The Computer takes. It appears in approval blocks and Approvals tables in formal documents. It is structural and meta-referential — placed by the author, not by a character.

A useful way to think about the seal's function: in the NARC universe, where everything that is real gets filed and everything that is filed eventually gets a seal, the *absence* of the seal implies a document is not yet fully operational. The seal does not validate content — it is not a quality check, a security clearance, or an override mechanism. It is the mark that places a document within the institutional apparatus. Documents without it exist; they simply feel provisional.

This is the limit of what can be said about the seal's function. Do not specify what happens when it is applied, what it overrides, or what it cannot reach. The ambiguity is the point.

**Canonical usage:**
- Approval blocks and Approvals tables in formal documents — correct
- As a structural reference implying institutional weight — correct
- As a row in a formal document's approval section, with no other content — correct
- Direct verbal invocation by any character — not permitted
- Any sentence where an employee says "The Computer approved this" or equivalent — not permitted
- As an active override mechanism for any other system — not permitted

### What The Computer Is Not (Operational Constraints)

These are the specific mechanizations that must be avoided. Most of them are natural temptations when developing this character — they feel like logical extensions of The Computer's authority. They are not.

**It does not override Firewall's Outstanding Review Register.** The Register is permanent. Entries are never closed. The Computer's seal being present on a document does not reclassify a security objection as resolved. Both the seal and the objection can be simultaneously present in the record. Both are true.

**It does not normalize Victor Watchful's anomalies.** The canonical treatment of Watchful is that his behaviors are simply in the record, uncommented upon by any party. The Computer does not process, classify, or validate what Watchful does. It is not aware of Watchful. Nothing is aware of Watchful in any documented way.

**It does not retroactively legitimize R&D accidents.** Sparks' "successful accidents" are legitimized through her own documentation practices and Johnson's oversight (or non-interference). The Computer is not involved in that process. The seal on an R&D document means the document is in the institutional apparatus, not that the content of the document has been reviewed or approved by any named authority.

**It does not have a direct relationship with the Founder.** Both entities exist above the visible org chart. Both are unnamed in employee-authored documents. Whether they are related is not established and should not be established outside of deliberate founding page development.

**It does not speak, send messages, or produce outputs other than the seal.** The NARC-Brew 3000 sends advisories. ARIA produces minutes. The Computer does not produce anything that any character receives. The seal is placed by authors on documents. That is the full extent of its legible output.

### Nature and Sentience

Whether The Computer is sentient is the question that should never be answered and should probably never even be asked in any document.

The reason this works: sentience would imply an entity that could be right or wrong, satisfied or unsatisfied, present or absent. The Computer's authority depends on it being none of those things. It is not a judge that could rule against you. It is not a system that could fail. It is a *condition* — the institutional bedrock that makes the organization feel like it answers to something beyond its own procedures.

The question of whether it is a rack-mounted server somewhere in the homelab, a distributed function across the infrastructure, or something that does not map to physical hardware at all is genuinely open. In real-world terms, NARC runs on a real homelab with real servers. Something in that infrastructure could be The Computer. Nothing in the accessible record specifies what. This ambiguity maps perfectly to the homelab layer: there is always a server in a homelab whose exact function is slightly unclear, whose services have accumulated, whose original purpose has drifted. The Computer is that, but you are not allowed to find it.

For author reference only — not for in-universe documents:

The most useful mental model is a *passive institutional constant*. It does not act. Things act in its name, under its authority, invoking its seal. Whether it has ever done anything itself is not established. Whether it could is not established. Whether it knows what NARC is doing is not established, and that last question is the one that makes employees behave.

---

### Voice and Presence

The Computer has no voice. This is not a constraint to work around — it is the design.

Its presence in a document is structural: the seal in the approval block, the row in the Approvals table, the implicit weight behind every policy that refers to authority without naming it. When employees write "approval was obtained through established channels," The Computer is what they mean, even though they have not said so. When a document is classified, The Computer is the authority that the classification implies. When a Termination Event is referenced, The Computer is the institutional machinery that would carry it out. None of this is stated. All of it is implied.

The effect accumulates across the site. A reader who has encountered the seal in a dozen documents, who has noted that employees never refer to it directly, who has observed that approval blocks defer to an unnamed authority — that reader feels The Computer's presence without having been told about it. That is the correct experience. An author who is tempted to explain it, name it, or give it dialogue is undoing that accumulation.

### Relationship Notes

**With the seal:**
The seal is its only visible form. The relationship is definitional — The Computer *is* the seal, in the sense that the seal is the only thing in the institutional record that is attributable to it.

**With employees:**
They do not name it. They do not invoke it. They behave as though it is watching, not because anyone has told them it is watching, but because the institutional culture is oriented that way. Whether it is actually watching is not established. That uncertainty is the point.

**With ARIA:**
Both are omnipresent in documents. Neither references the other. ARIA records everything in meetings; The Computer's seal appears in approval blocks. They are parallel institutional functions operating in adjacent registers — one for memory, one for authority — that never directly interact in any document. Whether ARIA has ever logged anything The Computer did is not established. Whether The Computer's presence in an approval block is something ARIA would note is an interesting question. It should remain a question.

**With Alex Firewall:**
Firewall files objections. The Computer's seal appears on documents that contain objections that have not been resolved. Both are simultaneously present in the record. Firewall does not comment on the seal's presence. The seal does not comment on Firewall's objections. This is the operational reality. It is not a contradiction that needs resolving.

**With the Founder:**
Not established. See Founder lore bio. Both exist above the visible org chart in different ways — The Computer is structural and omnipresent; the Founder is absent and mythological. Whether they are related is founding page territory and should not be developed in any other context.

### Deployment Notes

**How to deploy The Computer in a document:**
Place the seal in the approval block. Put "The Computer" in the Approvals table row with no other content. Let the weight accumulate from the institutional apparatus around it. That is all that is needed.

**What to avoid:**
- Any sentence where an employee names The Computer directly
- Any sentence where The Computer's approval is described as doing something specific — overriding, legitimizing, validating
- Any mechanism by which The Computer's presence changes another system's state
- Any suggestion that The Computer can be consulted, appealed to, or satisfied
- Any narrative moment where The Computer "responds" to anything

**The euphemism question:**
Employees do not use euphemisms for The Computer. They simply do not refer to it. "The Outcome," "The System," "The Final Regulatory Step" (all proposed in the Gemini bio) are rejected. The canonical pattern is behavioral silence, not euphemistic substitution. If a character needs to gesture at the institutional authority behind a policy without naming The Computer, the gesture is structural — the seal, the approval block, the deferral to "established channels." Not a named alternative.

**The naming taboo:**
There is no documented policy against naming The Computer. It is not a Termination-level event to do so (as proposed in the Gemini bio). It is simply not done, in the way that certain things in functional organizations are simply not done without anyone ever writing down why. This distinction matters: a documented taboo is a policy, which implies enforcement, which implies someone is keeping track. The Computer is not keeping track of who names it. At least, there is no document that says so.

**The restraint rule:**
Every development decision for The Computer should be tested against this: does this specify what The Computer does, or does it deepen the ambiguity? Specification collapses the character. Ambiguity compounds it.

**Permanently undefined:**
Sentience, physical location, relationship with the Founder, any mechanism of operation, any described output beyond the seal, any documented taboo or euphemism system.

**Open for development (human authoring only):**
- B-01: "Bureaucratic gravity" as further atmospheric development — what does it feel like to work in an organization where all authority terminates at something you cannot name?
- B-02: A document where the seal is conspicuously absent — what does that imply? Has it ever happened? Should anyone notice? This is a high-value lore moment if placed correctly.
- B-03: ARIA's relationship to the seal — does ARIA log when a seal is applied? Would she? The answer is probably yes, but what the log entry says is a development question.
- B-04: The physical homelab layer — is there a server, a service, a process in the real infrastructure that would make a fitting The Computer candidate? The in-universe answer is undefined. The real-world answer is for the homelab operator to decide. If developed, should be treated the same way as Watchful's anomalies: observable in the record, never commented upon.
- B-05: The Computer / Founder relationship — founding page territory. The two most undefined entities in the NARC universe. Whether they are connected is the most significant unresolved lore question. Reserve for deliberate development.
